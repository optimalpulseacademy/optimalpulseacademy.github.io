Step 1 of 7

US healthcare & insurance basics

Why it matters: Every claim depends on who pays and what the patient's plan covers. Get this wrong and nothing after it works.

What to learn

  • Medicare (Parts A, B, C, D), Medicaid, commercial plans, TRICARE, workers' comp
  • HMO vs PPO, referrals and prior authorization
  • Deductible, copay and coinsurance, and who owes what
  • HIPAA: keeping patient information private

Learn from the source

  • Medicare.gov: Medicare basics
  • HealthCare.gov: insurance glossary
  • HHS.gov: HIPAA

Check yourself

A plan pays 80% after the deductible and the patient pays the other 20%. What is that 20% called?

Coinsurance is a percentage of the allowed amount. A copay is a fixed dollar amount, and a premium is what the patient pays to keep the plan.

Which US law protects the privacy of patients' health information?

HIPAA's Privacy Rule sets the rules for protecting patient health information. Billers handle it every day.

How we help: From Zero to CPB: chapter 1 (Types of insurance) and chapter 6 (HIPAA and compliance).

Step 2 of 7

Medical words & code sets

Why it matters: Codes are the language of every claim. Billers read them every day; coders assign them.

What to learn

  • Medical terminology: prefixes, roots and suffixes
  • ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes: why the patient was seen
  • CPT procedure codes: what the provider did
  • HCPCS Level II: supplies, drugs and some services
  • Modifiers and how they change payment

Learn from the source

  • MedlinePlus (NIH): understanding medical words, free
  • CMS: ICD-10 codes and official guidelines
  • AMA: about CPT
  • CMS: HCPCS Level II

Check yourself

A patient is seen for a sprained ankle. Which code set tells the payer WHY the patient was seen?

ICD-10-CM codes are diagnoses: the reason for the visit. CPT describes what was done.

Who maintains HCPCS Level II codes?

CMS maintains HCPCS Level II. The AMA maintains CPT (also called HCPCS Level I).

How we help: From Zero to CPB: chapter 5 (Coding for billers). A full coding course is in development.

Step 3 of 7

The claim journey

Why it matters: Billing is a chain. Knowing every link lets you find exactly where a claim broke.

What to learn

  • Registration and eligibility checks before the visit
  • Charge entry from the visit to the claim
  • CMS-1500 (professional) and UB-04 (facility) claims
  • Clearinghouses, payer processing, EOBs and ERAs
  • Payment posting and patient statements

Learn from the source

  • NUCC: 1500 claim form instructions
  • CMS: Medicare Claims Processing Manual

Check yourself

A family doctor's office bills an office visit. Which claim form is used?

Physician and other professional services go on the CMS-1500 (or its electronic version). UB-04 is for hospitals and facilities; CMS-855I is a Medicare enrollment form.

Which step BEFORE the visit prevents many denials?

If the patient isn't covered, or the service needs authorization, you find out before the visit, not after the denial.

How we help: From Zero to CPB: chapter 3 (Claims and billing) and chapter 4 (Billing regulations).

Step 4 of 7

Denials, appeals & A/R follow-up

Why it matters: This is where billers earn their value: turning unpaid claims into payment.

What to learn

  • Reading denial reason codes on the EOB or remittance
  • Contractual write-offs vs patient responsibility
  • Corrected claims vs appeals, and their deadlines
  • NCCI edits that bundle services
  • Working the A/R aging report, oldest and largest first

Learn from the source

  • X12: claim adjustment and remark code lists
  • CMS: Original Medicare appeals
  • CMS: NCCI edits

Check yourself

A denial shows group code CO (contractual obligation). Can the practice bill that amount to the patient?

CO means the amount falls on the provider under the contract. PR (patient responsibility) is the group code for amounts the patient owes.

How many levels of appeal does Original Medicare (Parts A and B) have?

Redetermination, reconsideration, the ALJ hearing, the Medicare Appeals Council, then federal court: five levels.

How we help: From Zero to CPB: chapter 2 (Reimbursement and collections) and chapter 7 (Case analysis).

Step 5 of 7

Credentialing & enrollment

Why it matters: A provider who isn't enrolled can't be paid, however perfect the claim. Many credentialing roles are remote.

What to learn

  • Provider identifiers: NPI, tax ID, licenses, DEA
  • CAQH ProView profiles and attestations
  • Medicare enrollment (PECOS) and state Medicaid enrollment
  • Commercial payer applications and contracts
  • Follow-up, timelines, revalidation and re-credentialing

Learn from the source

  • CMS: NPPES (NPI registry)
  • CAQH ProView
  • CMS: PECOS (Medicare enrollment)

Check yourself

Where does a provider get an NPI?

NPIs are issued through NPPES, run for CMS. CAQH stores provider data that many commercial payers use.

Through which online system do providers enroll in Medicare?

PECOS is CMS's online Medicare enrollment system. Paper CMS-855 forms are the alternative.

Step 6 of 7

Exam prep & certification

Why it matters: A credential shows employers you know the whole cycle, not just one task.

What to learn

  • Choose your exam: CPB (billing), CPC (coding), or CPCS (credentialing)
  • Learn the exam format, domains and the books allowed
  • Study in short daily sessions; review your mistakes, not just your score
  • Take timed practice exams before the real one

Learn from the source

  • AAPC: CPB certification
  • AAPC: CPC certification
  • NAMSS: CPCS certification
  • Our free article: the CPB exam explained

Check yourself

What score do you need to pass the CPB exam?

70%, which is 95 of the 135 questions. Always confirm current details on AAPC's site before booking.

Which organization offers the CPCS credentialing certification?

The CPCS (Certified Provider Credentialing Specialist) is offered by NAMSS. AAPC offers the CPB and CPC.

How we help: From Zero to CPB: 235 practice questions, including a 135-question full exam with rationales. Our credentialing guide is a learning and job guide, not CPCS exam prep.

Step 7 of 7

Getting hired

Why it matters: Knowledge gets you the interview. Showing it clearly gets you the job.

What to learn

  • Start with an entry role: eligibility, charge entry, payment posting, A/R follow-up or credentialing assistant
  • Read 10 real job posts and list the skills they repeat
  • Write a one-page resume with only skills you can explain in an interview
  • Practice explaining a denial and how you would fix it, in clear English
  • Keep learning: payer rules and codes change every year
